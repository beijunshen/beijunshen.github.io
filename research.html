Research

My research lies at the intersection of solid mechanics, polymer physics, and advanced manufacturing. It advances the experimental, theoretical, and computational mechanics of polymeric materials and structures toward resilient and sustainable engineered systems. The central aim is to establish predictive relationships among processing, microstructure, time-dependent mechanical behavior, and structural performance, and to use them in design and manufacturing, with the time-dependent behavior of the material treated as a design variable. The work spans the life cycle of a polymeric part: design and manufacture for a prescribed response, reliable performance over time in service, and recycling into new load-bearing use.

Three connected directions pursue this aim. Constitutive models for polymer families inform structural design, designs define targets for manufacturing, and new printed or recycled materials provide data that extend the models. The applications range from impact protection and deployable aerospace structures to biomedical devices and the load-bearing use of recycled plastics.

1. Physics-Based Constitutive Discovery for Polymer Families

Predictive constitutive models for entire families of polymers, whose physical mechanisms and functional forms are identified from data by machine learning.

Constitutive models relate the stress in a material to its deformation history, and they underpin finite-element analysis and design. For polymers whose response is viscoelastic and viscoplastic, spans the glass transition, and depends on environmental conditions, such models are typically developed and calibrated for one material at a time, which limits how quickly new materials reach engineering use. This direction develops constitutive models for entire families of polymers, in which composition, crosslink density, or processing conditions enter as variables. Machine learning, including symbolic regression, identifies the physical mechanisms involved, such as network stretching, reptation, and glassy yield, together with their functional forms, so that the resulting models remain interpretable and can be implemented directly in finite-element codes. Once a model is established for a family, a new member can be characterized with a small number of tests. The long-term goal is an open library of physics-based constitutive models, organized by mechanism, that can be extended to new polymer families as they are introduced.

This direction builds on my postdoctoral work on digitally mixed polymers, printed voxel by voxel from a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer. I showed that temperature, frequency, and composition act jointly on the glass transition of the entire family, a time–temperature–composition equivalence, and on this basis developed a single finite-strain viscoelastic–viscoplastic framework for the family that predicts the response of a composition withheld from calibration. Photopolymers cured by digital light processing, in which the light dose controls crosslink density, are the next family to be studied.

Applications: rapid characterization of new and printed polymers; predictive simulation of polymeric components across loading rate and temperature; design of graded and multimaterial parts.

2. Programming Structures in Time

Structures whose response over time is designed by tuning the time scales of the material and of the structure together.

The function of architected structures often relies on geometric nonlinearity, such as buckling, snap-through, and folding, yet these structures are commonly designed with the material idealized as linear elastic or hyperelastic. A polymeric structure involves two sets of time scales: those of the material, set by its relaxation spectrum, and those of the structure, such as the rate at which an instability develops and the time required for a deformed region to recover. The ratio of a material relaxation time to a structural time scale acts as a structural Deborah number, which determines whether a region responds in a rubbery or a glassy manner during an instability. This direction studies how the two sets of time scales interact and how they can be tuned together. Spatial heterogeneity is known to broaden the relaxation spectrum of glass-forming polymers. In a printed structure, this heterogeneity can be prescribed: the local composition shifts and broadens the local relaxation spectrum, and the geometry determines how relaxation translates into load transfer, buckling, and recovery. The aim is structures with a designed time-dependent response, such as lattices whose energy absorption remains nearly constant over a wide range of impact speeds, or folded structures that deploy in a prescribed sequence after long-term storage, without sensors or actuators. The long-term goal is a set of design principles that relate material and structural time scales, and a design tool that translates a required response over time into composition and geometry.

This direction builds on my doctoral research on architected lattices made of liquid crystal elastomers, in which I showed that the viscoelasticity of the material alters the structural response. The interplay of nonlinear viscoelasticity and snap-through caused buckled layers to recover locally and partially, a path-dependent response that increased energy absorption. In a subsequent design, a stretchable elastomer bar that competes with beam buckling activated stretching and bending members in sequence and increased energy absorption by up to 2.5 times relative to a rigid bar.

Applications: reusable impact protection; energy absorption that is insensitive to impact speed; deployable aerospace structures; vibration isolation.

3. Advanced and Sustainable Manufacturing

Manufacturing routes, from multimaterial 3D printing to the processing of recycled feedstocks, linked to microstructure and mechanical performance.

Manufacturing and processing determine the microstructure on which the first two directions depend: the composition placed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, the crosslink density set by the light dose in digital light processing, and the morphology of blends and recyclates formed during melt extrusion. Post-consumer polyolefins in particular are rarely used in demanding structural applications, largely because their contaminants and processing histories vary from batch to batch. This direction relates the size, shape, and continuity of contaminant domains, together with crystallinity and molecular weight, to the anisotropic mechanical response, including ductility, and formulates constitutive models in terms of these variables. A small set of standard measurements, such as calorimetry, microscopy, and rheometry, could then be used to qualify a batch, and the same relations would identify processing conditions that produce a favorable morphology. Aging in service, which proceeds through chain scission and crosslinking, can be described with the same variables. The long-term goal is to make processing and recycling part of the design of polymeric structures, so that recycled feedstocks can be qualified and used in load-bearing applications with the same confidence as virgin materials.

This direction builds on my experience across manufacturing routes. As an undergraduate, I contributed to the development of a multi-axis rotary 3D printer for curved-layer fused deposition modeling, and at Columbia I design and print voxel-graded polymer structures. In my doctoral research on films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene, I showed that polyethylene domains drawn into elongated striations, rather than crystalline orientation, govern the anisotropic ductility, and that splitting and restacking the melt with a layer-multiplying element doubled the transverse ductility and restored the performance of virgin material with the same polyethylene content.

Applications: load-bearing parts from recycled plastics; graded and multimaterial components made by 3D printing; a more circular use of plastics.

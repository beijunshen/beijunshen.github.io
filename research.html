Research

My research advances the experimental, theoretical, and computational mechanics of polymeric materials and structures, coupled with advanced manufacturing, toward resilient and sustainable engineered systems. Its central aim is to predict the large, time-dependent mechanical response of polymeric materials and structures from their constituents, microstructure, and geometry, and to use these predictions to design and manufacture them, with the time-dependent behavior of the material treated as a design variable. My work to date draws on three classes of polymers: rubbery and glassy photopolymers mixed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, liquid crystal elastomers in architected lattices, and semicrystalline polyolefins, virgin and recycled, processed by melt extrusion. The three research directions below are coupled: constitutive models for polymer families inform structural design, designs define targets for manufacturing, and new printed or recycled materials provide data that extend the models.

Physics-Based Constitutive Discovery for Polymer Families

Constitutive models relate the stress in a material to its deformation history, and they underpin finite-element analysis and design. For polymers whose response is viscoelastic and viscoplastic and spans the glass transition, such models are typically developed and calibrated for one material at a time. In my postdoctoral work on digitally mixed polymers, printed voxel by voxel from a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer, I showed that temperature, frequency, and composition act jointly on the glass transition of the entire family, a time–temperature–composition equivalence. Building on this equivalence, I developed a single finite-strain viscoelastic–viscoplastic framework for the family that predicts the response of a composition withheld from calibration.

I aim to extend this approach by discovering physics-based constitutive models for polymer families from data, including their rate- and temperature-dependent viscoelastic and viscoplastic response. Machine learning, including symbolic regression, will identify the physical mechanisms involved, such as network stretching, reptation, and glassy yield, together with their functional forms, while composition or crosslink density enters through the material parameters. The resulting models remain interpretable and can be implemented directly in finite-element codes. Once a model is established for a family, a new member can be characterized with a small number of tests, which shortens the path from a new material to its use in engineering design. Photopolymers cured by digital light processing, in which the light dose controls crosslink density, are the next family to be studied.

Programming Structures in Time

The function of architected structures often relies on geometric nonlinearity, such as buckling, snap-through, and folding, yet these structures are commonly designed with the material idealized as linear elastic or hyperelastic. In my doctoral research on architected lattices made of liquid crystal elastomers, I showed that the viscoelasticity of the material alters the structural response. The interplay of nonlinear viscoelasticity and snap-through caused buckled layers to recover locally and partially, a path-dependent response that increased energy absorption. Introducing a stretchable elastomer bar that competes with beam buckling activated stretching and bending members in sequence and increased energy absorption by up to 2.5 times relative to a rigid bar.

Such structures involve two sets of time scales: those of the material, set by its relaxation spectrum, and those of the structure, such as the rate at which an instability develops and the time required for a deformed region to recover. Their ratio acts as a structural Deborah number, which determines whether a region responds in a rubbery or a glassy manner during an instability. I study how these two sets of time scales interact and how they can be tuned together. Spatial heterogeneity is known to broaden the relaxation spectrum of glass-forming polymers; in a printed structure, such heterogeneity can be prescribed, because the local composition shifts and broadens the local relaxation spectrum, while the geometry determines how relaxation translates into load transfer, buckling, and recovery. The aim is structures with a designed time-dependent response, such as lattices whose energy absorption remains nearly constant over a wide range of impact speeds, or folded structures that deploy in a prescribed sequence after long-term storage, without sensors or actuators.

Advanced and Sustainable Manufacturing

Manufacturing and processing determine the microstructure on which the first two directions depend: the composition placed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, the crosslink density set by the light dose in digital light processing, and the morphology of blends and recyclates formed during melt extrusion. As an undergraduate, I contributed to the development of a multi-axis rotary 3D printer for curved-layer fused deposition modeling, and at Columbia I design and print voxel-graded polymer structures. In my doctoral research on films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene, I showed that polyethylene domains drawn into elongated striations, rather than crystalline orientation, govern anisotropic failure. Splitting and restacking the melt with a layer-multiplying element doubled the transverse ductility and restored the performance of virgin material with the same polyethylene content.

Post-consumer polyolefins are rarely used in demanding structural applications, largely because their contaminants and processing histories vary from batch to batch. I aim to relate the size, shape, and continuity of contaminant domains, together with crystallinity and molecular weight, to the anisotropic mechanical response, including ductility, and to formulate constitutive models in terms of these variables. A small set of standard measurements, such as calorimetry, microscopy, and rheometry, could then be used to qualify a batch, and the same relations would identify processing conditions that produce a favorable morphology. Aging in service, through chain scission and crosslinking, can be incorporated through the same variables. Together, these advances would allow recycled polyolefins to be used in load-bearing parts and support a more circular use of plastics.

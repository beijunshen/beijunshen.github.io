Research

Polymeric materials and structures are everywhere in engineering, from protective equipment to aircraft, medical devices, and soft robots. Their response is large, nonlinear, and dissipative; it changes with rate, temperature, and load history, and it is set by microstructure and by the structure built from them. Yet constitutive modeling still largely works one model for one material. My research asks whether the mechanical response of polymeric materials and structures can be predicted before they are made, from a few measurable microstructural variables and the deformation mechanisms polymers share. Three areas pursue that question together, and they converge on a predictive digital twin of polymeric materials and structures.

Constitutive discovery for polymer families

Multimaterial PolyJet printing mixes a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer voxel by voxel into a continuous family of polymers, rubbery to glassy. Each mixture used to be treated as its own material. I established a time–temperature–composition equivalence for this family: temperature and frequency translate the glass transition, composition translates and broadens it, and the storage modulus of every composition collapses onto one master curve. On that basis I built a single finite-strain constitutive framework for the entire family, an equilibrium network with rate-dependent reptational, intermolecular, and glassy mechanisms connected by composition scaling laws. It captures the nonlinear, rate-dependent response of every calibrated composition and predicts a mixture withheld from calibration: one model for an entire material family, with composition a continuous design variable.

Next: discovering the constitutive laws of polymer families from data, with machine learning that selects among physical mechanisms rather than fitting a black box.

Structural design for energy absorption

Helmets and armor need materials that absorb more energy the faster they are loaded and recover for reuse. During my Ph.D. I coupled architected lattices, which trap energy by buckling, with liquid crystal elastomers, which dissipate through viscoelasticity and a rate-dependent soft stress. In a bistable lattice, energy absorption rose over two orders of magnitude with strain rate, and my simulations showed that viscoelasticity makes stacked layers buckle nonuniformly, adding dissipation that grows with layer count, graded beam thickness, and strain rate. Adding a stretchable elastomer bar that competes with beam buckling, I found the thickness ratio at which the lattice absorbs up to 2.5 times the energy of a rigid-bar design: bending and stretching members can be activated in sequence by tuning geometry and material.

Next: designing material and geometry together, so that structures respond on the time scales they meet, from impact protection to deployable structures.

Processing and mechanics of recycled polymers

Polyolefins are held back from recycling because post-consumer feedstocks vary and carry contaminants, chiefly polyethylene, that degrade mechanical performance. I led a mechanistic study of films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene, quantifying their polyethylene by calorimetry. Extrusion draws the polyethylene into elongated striations, and by microscopy, rheometry, tensile tests, and scattering I showed that these striations, not crystalline orientation, govern anisotropic failure. Splitting and restacking the melt refined the striations, doubled transverse ductility, and restored the performance of virgin material with the same polyethylene content.

Next: predicting the stiffness, strength, and ductility of recycled and aged polymers from a few measurements of their microstructure.

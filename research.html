Research

Polymers carry load in helmet liners and body armor, aircraft and spacecraft components, medical devices, and soft robots. Their response is large, nonlinear, and dissipative; it depends on strain rate, temperature, and loading history, and it is set by the microstructure that composition and processing produce and by the geometry of the structure. My research combines experiments, constitutive modeling, finite-element simulation, and machine learning to understand and predict this response and to use it in design. I work with three classes of polymers: rubbery and glassy photopolymers mixed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, liquid crystal elastomers built into architected lattices, and semicrystalline polyolefins, virgin and recycled, shaped by melt extrusion. The three directions below share one approach: measurements calibrate constitutive laws, simulations with those laws guide design, and the manufactured parts are tested to refine the laws.

Accelerated Constitutive Discovery for Polymer Families

Constitutive models connect a polymer’s microstructure to the stress it carries, and finite-element design rests on them. For polymers that are viscoelastic and viscoplastic and pass through the glass transition, these models are still built one material at a time: a functional form is proposed and calibrated to that material. Data-driven methods, from neural-network models to automated discovery from libraries of strain-energy functions, have made calibration far faster. I focus on extending model discovery to the rate- and temperature-dependent response of whole polymer families: closed-form laws assembled from physical mechanisms such as network stretching, reptation, and glassy yield, in which composition or crosslink density enters through the parameters. Machine learning, including symbolic regression, selects the mechanisms and their functional forms from the data, so that the resulting law can be read, tested against new experiments, and implemented in finite-element codes. With one law for a family, a new member is characterized with a few tests instead of a full test campaign, which shortens the path from a new material to its use in design.

Current work. Multimaterial PolyJet printing mixes a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer into a continuous family of materials. I showed that temperature, frequency, and composition act together on the glass transition of this family, a time–temperature–composition equivalence, and built a single finite-strain viscoelastic–viscoplastic framework that predicts the response of a composition withheld from calibration. The next family is photopolymers cured by digital light processing, in which light dose sets crosslink density.

Coupling Material and Structural Time Scales

Architected structures owe their function to geometric nonlinearity: beams buckle, shells snap through, and folds open. They are usually designed with the material treated as linear elastic or hyperelastic. The polymers they are made of are viscoelastic, so such a structure has two sets of time scales: those of the material, set by its relaxation spectrum, and those of the structure, such as how quickly an instability develops and how long a deformed region takes to recover. I study how the two interact and how they can be tuned together. Heterogeneity is known to broaden relaxation in glasses at the molecular scale; in a printed structure it can be placed deliberately, since composition printed voxel by voxel shifts and broadens the local relaxation spectrum, and the geometry then decides how that relaxation turns into load transfer, buckling, and recovery. The aim is structures whose response over time is designed, such as lattices that absorb nearly the same energy over a wide range of impact speeds, or folded structures that deploy in a set order after long storage, without sensors or actuators.

Current work. In architected liquid crystal elastomer lattices, nonlinear viscoelasticity and snap-through made buckled layers recover locally and partially, and this path-dependent response increased energy absorption. Adding a stretchable elastomer bar that competes with beam buckling, I identified the geometry at which bending and stretching members activate in sequence, raising energy absorption up to 2.5 times relative to a rigid bar.

Advanced and Sustainable Manufacturing

Manufacturing sets the microstructure on which the first two directions build: composition placed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, crosslink density set by light dose in digital light processing, and the morphology of blends and recyclates formed in melt extrusion. My current focus in this direction is recycled polyolefins. Post-consumer polyolefins are rarely used in load-bearing parts. Each batch carries different contaminants, mainly polyethylene in polypropylene, and has a different processing history, so stiffness, ductility, and failure vary from batch to batch. I relate the size, shape, and continuity of contaminant domains, together with crystallinity and molecular weight, to anisotropic failure, and build constitutive and failure models in these variables. Measurements that recyclers can make routinely, such as calorimetry, microscopy, and rheometry, would then be enough to qualify a batch, and the same relations indicate the processing conditions that produce a favorable morphology. Aging in service enters the same variables through chain scission and crosslinking.

Current work. In films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene, I showed that polyethylene drawn into elongated striations, rather than crystalline orientation, governs anisotropic failure, and that splitting and restacking the melt with a layer-multiplying element doubled transverse ductility and restored the performance of virgin material with the same polyethylene content.

Research

My research lies at the intersection of solid mechanics, polymeric materials, and advanced manufacturing. It establishes predictive relationships among processing, microstructure, time-dependent mechanical behavior, and structural performance, and uses them to design and manufacture polymeric materials and structures for resilient and sustainable engineered systems. The work spans the life cycle of a polymeric part: design and manufacture for a prescribed response, reliable performance over time in service, and recycling into new load-bearing use.

The work is organized in three connected directions, each combining experiment, theory, and computation. The experiments include large-deformation mechanical testing across strain rates and temperatures, dynamic mechanical analysis, and digital image correlation; the theory and computation include constitutive modeling, finite-element simulation with user-defined material models, and machine learning; and multimaterial 3D printing produces many of the materials and structures studied.

Together, the three directions form a predictive digital twin of polymeric materials and structures. The digital twin supports forward prediction, from processing to structural performance, and inverse design, from a target performance to the structural design, constituent material, and processing route that achieve the target. Measurements on printed and recycled materials and on manufactured structures, in turn, refine the constitutive models. The applications range from impact protection, deployable aerospace structures, and infrastructure components to biomedical devices and the load-bearing use of recycled plastics.

1. Physics-Based Constitutive Discovery for Polymer Families

Physics-based constitutive models for entire families of polymers, developed by combining physical insight with machine learning.

Constitutive models relate the stress in a material to its deformation history, and they underpin finite-element analysis and design. Polymers undergo large, nonlinear deformation, and their viscoelastic and viscoplastic behavior depends on loading rate and temperature, changes markedly through the glass transition, and evolves with exposure to light, moisture, and other environmental conditions. Constitutive models for such behavior are typically developed and calibrated for one material at a time, a practice that limits how quickly new materials reach engineering use. Data-driven methods have accelerated the identification of constitutive models, but most have addressed hyperelastic behavior, in which the stress depends only on the current deformation. Time- and history-dependent behavior is considerably more challenging, because the stress depends on the history of deformation through internal variables: both the constitutive relations and the evolution laws of these variables must be identified from data that span a range of loading rates and temperatures. The difficulty is greatest at the large, nonlinear deformations that many polymeric components undergo in service. Models represented by neural networks or other general-purpose function approximators also offer limited physical interpretation.

This direction differs in three respects. First, it addresses time- and history-dependent behavior at large, nonlinear deformation, including viscoelasticity and viscoplasticity through the glass transition. Second, it seeks one physics-based model for an entire family of polymers, in which composition, crosslink density, or processing conditions enter as variables, in place of one model per material. Third, it combines physical insight with machine learning, including symbolic regression, to identify the governing physical mechanisms, such as entropic stretching of the molecular network, intermolecular resistance to yielding in the glassy state, and molecular relaxation by reptation, together with internal variables that describe how the microstructure evolves with deformation and environmental exposure, such as molecular orientation, free volume, and crosslink density. Closed-form expressions are sought for both the constitutive relations and the evolution laws of these internal variables, so that the resulting models remain interpretable and thermodynamically consistent and can be implemented directly in finite-element codes.

Models of this kind require data that span composition, processing, loading, and environmental conditions, and collecting such data one specimen at a time is slow. This direction therefore develops high-throughput characterization for polymer families: specimen libraries printed with systematically varied composition or processing conditions, automated mechanical testing under controlled loading and environmental conditions, and graded specimens in which full-field strain measurement captures the response of many compositions in a single test. Machine learning can then guide the choice of subsequent experiments, so that each new test adds as much information to the model as possible. The long-term goal is an open, modular library of physics-based constitutive models, in which each mechanism is described by a module that can be calibrated, combined with others, and transferred across polymer families. With such a library, the constitutive description of a new polymer family, or of a known family under new environmental conditions, could be assembled from existing modules and calibrated with a small number of tests. Such a library would accelerate the discovery of constitutive models for a wide range of polymers and environmental conditions.

Applications: accelerated development and qualification of new polymers; predictive simulation of polymeric components in protective equipment, aerospace structures, medical devices, and soft robots; prediction of how mechanical behavior changes with exposure to light, moisture, and heat; design of graded and multimaterial parts.

This direction builds on my postdoctoral work on digitally mixed polymers, printed voxel by voxel from a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer. Combining large-deformation compression tests over three orders of magnitude in strain rate with dynamic mechanical analysis, I showed that the family follows a time–temperature–composition equivalence: the storage modulus of compositions from rubbery to glassy collapses onto a single master curve across temperature and frequency. On this basis, I formulated a single finite-strain viscoelastic–viscoplastic constitutive framework for the whole family, in which the material parameters scale with composition, implemented it for finite-element analysis, and showed that it predicts the response of a composition withheld from calibration across strain rates. A collaborative study on the same materials used data-driven methods to discover composition-dependent constitutive models [García-Ávila et al., arXiv 2026]. Mechanism-based formulations of this kind provide the starting point for discovery with machine learning.

2. Programming Structures in Time

Co-design of the time scales of the material and those of the structural response, to control how quickly and in what sequence a structure responds to mechanical loads or environmental stimuli and to realize prescribed functions over time.

The function of architected structures often relies on elastic instabilities, such as buckling and snap-through, and on other large, geometrically nonlinear deformations, such as folding. These structures are commonly designed with the material idealized as linear elastic or hyperelastic, an idealization that omits the rate and history dependence of polymeric materials. A polymeric structure involves two sets of time scales: those of the material, characterized by its relaxation times, and those of the structural response, set by the duration of loading and by the speed at which an instability develops. Whether a region of the structure responds in a relaxed, rubbery manner or in an unrelaxed, glassy manner depends on how these two sets of time scales compare. This direction studies how they interact and how they can be designed together, so that the speed and sequence of the structural response to a load or to an environmental stimulus, such as heat, light, or moisture, become design targets. In a multimaterial structure, the local material sets the local relaxation behavior, and the geometry determines how relaxation translates into load transfer, buckling, and recovery. Delayed and partial recovery of deformed regions can likewise be designed, for example to hold a deformed configuration and release it after a prescribed time.

Target functions include impact protection with energy absorption tailored to the range of impact speeds a structure must withstand, deployable structures that unfold in a prescribed sequence, vibration damping over a chosen frequency range, timed gripping and locomotion in soft robots, and multifunctional structures that carry load while dissipating energy or recovering their shape. Graded polymers with a designed rate-dependent response are also relevant to soft biomedical devices such as implants. The long-term goal is a set of design principles that relate material and structural time scales, together with computational design tools, built on the constitutive models of the first direction, that translate a required response over time into the distribution of materials and the geometry of a structure.

Applications: impact protection for people, vehicles, and equipment; deployable structures for aerospace; vibration damping; soft robots with timed gripping and locomotion; multifunctional load-bearing structures; soft biomedical devices.

This direction builds on my doctoral research on architected lattices made of liquid crystal elastomers. Combining finite-element simulations based on viscoelastic constitutive models with experiments, I showed that, because of the viscoelasticity of the material, the buckling sequence of stacked layers depends on the strain rate: the layers buckled nonuniformly at a low strain rate and simultaneously at a high strain rate. The interplay of nonlinear viscoelasticity and snap-through caused buckled layers to recover locally and partially, a path-dependent response that increased energy absorption. In a subsequent study, I added a stretchable liquid crystal elastomer bar to the unit cell of the lattice, so that bar stretching and beam bending were activated in sequence; this sequential activation increased energy absorption by up to 2.5 times relative to a design with a rigid bar. In ongoing work, presented at the 2026 Society of Engineering Science Annual Technical Meeting, I am designing fiber networks that store and dissipate energy, using multimaterial 3D printing of viscoelastic polymers.

3. Advanced and Sustainable Manufacturing

Mechanics-based characterization and modeling of processing–structure–property relations for multimaterial 3D-printed and recycled polymers.

Manufacturing and processing determine the microstructure on which the constitutive models of the first direction and the designs of the second depend: the composition placed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, the crosslink density set by the light dose in digital light processing, and the morphology, crystallinity, and molecular orientation of blends and recycled polymers formed during melt processing. For printed polymers, this direction connects printing parameters, such as the composition field and the light dose, to the resulting microstructure and constitutive behavior, including process-induced anisotropy and the mechanical behavior of interfaces between dissimilar materials, so that the models and designs can be realized as manufactured parts with predictable properties. The same printing processes produce the specimen libraries used for high-throughput characterization in the first direction.

For recycled polymers, this direction starts with post-consumer polyolefins, which make up a large share of plastic waste but are rarely used in demanding structural applications, largely because their composition, contamination, and thermomechanical history vary from batch to batch. It develops characterization methods and predictive tools, including constitutive models, that relate processing conditions to the microstructure, including the morphology of minor polymer phases and contaminants, crystallinity, and molecular weight, and relate the microstructure to the resulting mechanical response, including its anisotropy. These relations can guide the choice of processing conditions and could allow a batch to be qualified from a small set of routine measurements, such as calorimetry, microscopy, and rheometry. The same methods and models can be extended to aging in service under heat and ultraviolet light, which alters the molecular structure through chain scission and crosslinking. The long-term goal is to support the upcycling of plastic waste into load-bearing products and a more circular plastics economy, with processing and manufacturing chosen on the basis of predicted mechanical performance.

Applications: load-bearing products from recycled plastics, such as automotive, construction, and packaging components; rapid quality screening of recycled feedstocks; processing design for the upcycling of plastic waste; graded and multimaterial components made by additive manufacturing.

This direction builds on my experience with both printed and recycled polymers. As an undergraduate, I designed and developed a multi-axis rotary 3D printer for curved-layer fused deposition modeling, and at Columbia I design and print voxel-graded polymer structures. In my doctoral research, I characterized films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene to establish their processing–structure–property relations. Combining calorimetry, electron microscopy, rheometry, tensile testing, and X-ray scattering, I showed that polyethylene domains drawn into elongated striations govern the anisotropic ductility of these films. Films extruded with a layer-multiplying element, which splits and restacks the melt, had approximately twice the transverse ductility and matched the ductility of virgin material with the same polyethylene content.

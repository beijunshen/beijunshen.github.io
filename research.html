Research

Polymers carry load in helmet liners and body armor, aircraft and spacecraft components, medical devices, and soft robots. Their response is large, nonlinear, and dissipative; it depends on strain rate, temperature, and loading history, and it is set by the microstructure that composition and processing produce and by the geometry of the structure. My research combines experiments, constitutive modeling, finite-element simulation, and machine learning to understand and predict this response and to use it in design. I work with three classes of polymers: rubbery and glassy photopolymers mixed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, liquid crystal elastomers built into architected lattices, and semicrystalline polyolefins, virgin and recycled, shaped by melt extrusion. The three directions below share one approach: measurements calibrate constitutive laws, simulations with those laws guide design, and the manufactured parts are tested to refine the laws.

Physics-Based Constitutive Discovery for Polymer Families

Constitutive models connect a polymer’s microstructure to the stress it carries, and finite-element design rests on them. For polymers that are viscoelastic and viscoplastic and pass through the glass transition, these models are still built one material at a time. In my postdoctoral work on digitally mixed polymers, printed voxel by voxel from a rubbery and a glassy photopolymer, I showed that temperature, frequency, and composition act together on the glass transition of the whole family, a time–temperature–composition equivalence, and built one finite-strain viscoelastic–viscoplastic framework that predicts the response of a composition withheld from calibration. The same law now predicts new printed structures, such as wavy-fiber composites, without refitting. Building on this, I aim to discover physics-based laws from data: machine learning, including symbolic regression, selects physical mechanisms such as network stretching, reptation, and glassy yield and finds their functional forms, while composition or crosslink density enters through the parameters. With one law for a family, a new member is characterized with a few tests instead of a full test campaign, which shortens the path from a new material to its use in design. Photopolymers cured by digital light processing, in which light dose sets crosslink density, are the next family.

Programming Structures in Time

Architected structures owe their function to geometric nonlinearity: beams buckle, shells snap through, and folds open. They are usually designed with the material treated as linear elastic or hyperelastic. During my Ph.D., I combined architected lattices with liquid crystal elastomers and found that the material changes what the structure does. Nonlinear viscoelasticity and snap-through made buckled layers recover locally and partially, a path-dependent response that increased energy absorption; and a stretchable elastomer bar that competes with beam buckling activated bending and stretching members in sequence, raising energy absorption up to 2.5 times relative to a rigid bar. Such a structure has two sets of time scales: those of the material, set by its relaxation spectrum, and those of the structure, such as how quickly an instability develops and how long a deformed region takes to recover. I study how the two interact and how they can be tuned together. Heterogeneity is known to broaden relaxation in glasses at the molecular scale; in a printed structure it can be placed deliberately, since composition printed voxel by voxel shifts and broadens the local relaxation spectrum, and the geometry then decides how that relaxation turns into load transfer, buckling, and recovery. The aim is structures whose response over time is designed, such as lattices that absorb nearly the same energy over a wide range of impact speeds, or folded structures that deploy in a set order after long storage, without sensors or actuators.

Advanced and Sustainable Manufacturing

Manufacturing sets the microstructure on which the first two directions build: composition placed voxel by voxel in multimaterial 3D printing, crosslink density set by light dose in digital light processing, and the morphology of blends and recyclates formed in melt extrusion. As an undergraduate I contributed to a multi-axis rotary 3D printer for curved-layer fused deposition modeling, and at Columbia I design and print voxel-graded polymer structures. During my Ph.D., I studied films extruded from post-consumer polypropylene and showed that polyethylene drawn into elongated striations, rather than crystalline orientation, governs anisotropic failure, and that splitting and restacking the melt with a layer-multiplying element doubled transverse ductility and restored the performance of virgin material with the same polyethylene content. Post-consumer polyolefins are still rarely used in load-bearing parts, because each batch carries different contaminants and has a different processing history. I aim to relate the size, shape, and continuity of contaminant domains, together with crystallinity and molecular weight, to anisotropic failure, and to build constitutive and failure models in these variables. Measurements that recyclers can make routinely, such as calorimetry, microscopy, and rheometry, would then be enough to qualify a batch, and the same relations would indicate the processing conditions that produce a favorable morphology. Aging in service enters the same variables through chain scission and crosslinking.

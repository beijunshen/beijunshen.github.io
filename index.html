Polymers are among the most versatile materials in nature and engineering. Proteins and polysaccharides form the soft tissues of the human body, from skin and tendon to blood vessels and cartilage, and synthetic polymers are used in products ranging from tires, seals, and packaging to protective equipment, aircraft and spacecraft components, biomedical devices, and soft robots. They are lightweight, can be manufactured into complex shapes, and offer properties that can be tailored over orders of magnitude.

Their mechanical behavior is also among the most complex of any class of materials: polymers undergo large, nonlinear, and time-dependent deformation that depends on strain rate, temperature, and loading history, and that evolves with environmental conditions such as moisture, light, and aging. This behavior arises from the interplay of the constituent chemistry, the microstructure into which the constituents are organized, and the geometry of the structure, all of which are shaped by processing and manufacturing. Capturing this interplay in predictive models and using it in design remain central challenges in mechanics, and addressing them requires connecting the polymer physics that sets the behavior of the material, the mechanics that governs the structure, and the manufacturing that realizes both. Progress on these challenges would shorten the development of new materials, enable lighter and more resilient structures, and allow recycled polymers to be used where mechanical performance matters.

My research lies at the intersection of solid mechanics, polymer physics, and advanced manufacturing. It advances the experimental, theoretical, and computational mechanics of polymeric materials and structures toward resilient and sustainable engineered systems. The goal is to establish predictive relationships among processing, microstructure, time-dependent mechanical behavior, and structural performance, and to derive from them design principles for polymeric materials and structures across their life cycle: manufactured for a prescribed response, reliable over time in service, and recyclable into new load-bearing use. A unifying theme is that the time-dependent behavior of the material is treated as a design variable: constituents, microstructure, and geometry are chosen together, guided by predictive constitutive models and realized through advanced manufacturing. The work is organized in three connected directions:

  • Physics-based constitutive discovery: constitutive models for entire families of polymers, with composition or processing as variables, whose mechanisms and functional forms are identified from data by machine learning, so that a new material can be characterized with few experiments.
  • Programming structures in time: structures in which the viscoelastic time scales of the material and the time scales of structural instabilities are designed together, so that the time-dependent response of the structure, such as energy absorption across loading rates or sequential deployment, can be programmed.
  • Advanced and sustainable manufacturing: multimaterial additive manufacturing and melt processing of recycled polymers, in which processing conditions are linked to microstructure and mechanical performance in order to realize these designs and to qualify recycled feedstocks for structural use.

The three directions are coupled: constitutive models inform structural design, designs define targets for manufacturing, and manufactured materials and structures provide data that refine the models.

  • October 2026
    Excited to share that my second postdoc work at Columbia is now on arXiv: “Time–temperature–composition equivalence of the viscoelastic behavior through the glass transition of digitally mixed 3D-printed polymers”. Classical time–temperature equivalence (TTE) describes the linear viscoelastic behavior of a polymer by a shift along the frequency axis. In digitally mixed polymers made by multimaterial 3D printing, however, composition does more than shift the curves: it moves and broadens the glass transition and raises the rubbery modulus, so each mixture had to be treated on its own. Here, we establish a much more general time–temperature–composition equivalence (TTCE) for the entire printed family. Its closed form, a stretched exponential in reduced frequency, gives the stiffness and damping of the family across composition, temperature, and frequency. (LinkedIn post)
  • September 2026
    Excited to share that my first postdoc work with Prof. Mary C. Boyce is now on arXiv, after countless rounds of iteration and revision: “Composition-dependent nonlinear viscoelastic–viscoplastic behavior and constitutive framework for digitally mixed polymers spanning the glass transition”. We proposed a unified finite-strain constitutive framework that describes the whole family of digitally mixed polymers for multi-material 3D printing, from rubbery to glassy, and predicts a mixture we held back from calibration. It provides the fundamental tool for understanding these materials and a predictive basis for designing graded multi-material structures. Very proud of this one. Our collaborative paper with Prof. Adrián Buganza-Tepole’s group on data-driven discovery of composition-dependent constitutive models for the same materials is also out. More papers are on the way, stay tuned! (LinkedIn post)

About

I am a Postdoctoral Research Scientist in Mechanical Engineering at Columbia University, working with Prof. Mary C. Boyce on experiments and constitutive modeling of digitally mixed, multimaterial 3D-printed polymers and on the mechanics-guided design of bio-inspired graded composites and fiber networks. I received my Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering from Johns Hopkins University in 2025, advised by Prof. Vicky Nguyen and Prof. Sung Hoon Kang. My doctoral research addressed the mechanics and design of viscoelastic liquid crystal elastomer architected materials for energy absorption, and the processing–structure–property relations of recycled polyolefins. I received my B.S. in Mechanical Engineering from Shanghai Jiao Tong University in 2019.

My research develops predictive mechanics for polymeric materials and structures and connects it with advanced manufacturing. Polymers carry load in protective equipment, aerospace structures, medical devices, and soft robots. Their large, rate- and temperature-dependent deformation determines how a part absorbs energy, holds its shape, and fails, and this response is set by the microstructure and geometry that manufacturing produces. Combining experiments, constitutive theory, finite-element simulation, and machine learning, I pursue three connected directions, shown in the figure below. Physics-based constitutive discovery seeks one law for each polymer family, so that a new material can be described from a few tests. Programming structures in time tunes the time scales of the material and of the structure together, so that the response of a structure over time becomes a design variable. Advanced and sustainable manufacturing, from multimaterial 3D printing to recycled feedstocks, realizes these designs and supplies new materials and data. The three directions feed one another: constitutive laws enable design, designs set targets for manufacturing, and manufactured materials and parts return data that refine the laws.

  • October 2026
    Excited to share that my second postdoc work at Columbia is now on arXiv: “Time–temperature–composition equivalence of the viscoelastic behavior through the glass transition of digitally mixed 3D-printed polymers”. Classical time–temperature equivalence (TTE) describes the linear viscoelastic behavior of a polymer by a shift along the frequency axis. In digitally mixed polymers made by multimaterial 3D printing, however, composition does more than shift the curves: it moves and broadens the glass transition and raises the rubbery modulus, so each mixture had to be treated on its own. Here, we establish a much more general time–temperature–composition equivalence (TTCE) for the entire printed family. Its closed form, a stretched exponential in reduced frequency, gives the stiffness and damping of the family across composition, temperature, and frequency. (LinkedIn post)
  • September 2026
    Excited to share that my first postdoc work with Prof. Mary C. Boyce is now on arXiv, after countless rounds of iteration and revision: “Composition-dependent nonlinear viscoelastic–viscoplastic behavior and constitutive framework for digitally mixed polymers spanning the glass transition”. We proposed a unified finite-strain constitutive framework that describes the whole family of digitally mixed polymers for multi-material 3D printing, from rubbery to glassy, and predicts a mixture we held back from calibration. It provides the fundamental tool for understanding these materials and a predictive basis for designing graded multi-material structures. Very proud of this one. Our collaborative paper with Prof. Adrián Buganza-Tepole’s group on data-driven discovery of composition-dependent constitutive models for the same materials is also out. More papers are on the way, stay tuned! (LinkedIn post)

Polymers are among the most versatile materials in nature and engineering, from the soft tissues of the human body to tires, protective equipment, aircraft and spacecraft components, biomedical devices, and soft robots. Their mechanical behavior is also among the most complex: deformation is large, nonlinear, and time dependent, and it is governed by a microstructure set largely by processing and manufacturing. Predictive models of this behavior are needed to design lighter and more resilient structures, to bring new polymers into use more quickly, and to use recycled polymers in load-bearing applications.

My research lies at the intersection of solid mechanics, polymeric materials, and advanced manufacturing. It establishes predictive relationships among processing, microstructure, time-dependent mechanical behavior, and structural performance, and derives from them design principles across the life cycle of a polymeric part, toward resilient and sustainable engineered systems. The work is organized in three connected directions:

  • Physics-based constitutive discovery: constitutive models, the relations between stress and the history of deformation, for entire families of polymers, identified by combining physical insight with machine learning.
  • Programming structures in time: co-design of material and structural time scales to control how quickly and in what sequence a structure responds to loads or stimuli, for functions such as impact protection, sequential deployment, and timed motion in soft robots.
  • Advanced and sustainable manufacturing: mechanics-based characterization and modeling of processing–structure–property relations for 3D-printed and recycled polymers, toward the upcycling of plastic waste into load-bearing products.

Together, the three directions form a predictive digital twin of polymeric materials and structures: constitutive models inform structural design, designs define targets for manufacturing, and manufactured materials and structures provide data that refine the models.

  • October 2026
    Excited to share that my second postdoc work at Columbia is now on arXiv: “Time–temperature–composition equivalence of the viscoelastic behavior through the glass transition of digitally mixed 3D-printed polymers”. Classical time–temperature equivalence (TTE) describes the linear viscoelastic behavior of a polymer by a shift along the frequency axis. In digitally mixed polymers made by multimaterial 3D printing, however, composition does more than shift the curves: it moves and broadens the glass transition and raises the rubbery modulus, so each mixture had to be treated on its own. Here, we establish a much more general time–temperature–composition equivalence (TTCE) for the entire printed family. Its closed form, a stretched exponential in reduced frequency, gives the stiffness and damping of the family across composition, temperature, and frequency. (LinkedIn post)
  • September 2026
    Excited to share that my first postdoc work with Prof. Mary C. Boyce is now on arXiv, after countless rounds of iteration and revision: “Composition-dependent nonlinear viscoelastic–viscoplastic behavior and constitutive framework for digitally mixed polymers spanning the glass transition”. We proposed a unified finite-strain constitutive framework that describes the whole family of digitally mixed polymers for multi-material 3D printing, from rubbery to glassy, and predicts a mixture we held back from calibration. It provides the fundamental tool for understanding these materials and a predictive basis for designing graded multi-material structures. Very proud of this one. Our collaborative paper with Prof. Adrián Buganza-Tepole’s group on data-driven discovery of composition-dependent constitutive models for the same materials is also out. More papers are on the way, stay tuned! (LinkedIn post)
